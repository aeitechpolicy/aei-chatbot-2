	AI Pessimism Fades as Reality Takes Hold | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

While senior business leaders are increasingly bullish on AI’s potential to boost productivity, the broader American public remains skeptical. Only 17 percent believe AI will have a positive effect on the United States over the next two decades, and nearly two-thirds expect it will reduce the number of jobs. In a Pew survey of 25 nations, Americans were the most AI-anxious, with half saying they were more concerned than excited about AI’s spread.
That anxiety is often framed as a binary: “never surrender” or “resistance is futile”. But that framing misses the point. AI is coming to most workplaces, not as a sudden replacement but through gradual integration. The question is not whether AI will be used but how.
Yet the depth of America’s pessimism depends on what that “how” looks like in practice. In a large-scale Harvard Business School survey of 2,357 adults evaluating AI usage in 940 occupations, they found that reactions depend on how AI’s role is described. When AI was presented as a tool that augments rather than replaces human labor, a majority of Americans supported its use in 94 percent of occupations. Even when AI was described as fully automating core job tasks, respondents favored its use in 58 percent of occupations if it could outperform humans at a lower cost.
The key is specificity. When AI’s potential benefits are explained concretely—faster diagnoses, fewer repetitive tasks and injuries, better scheduling—attitudes shift from fear to interest. In the abstract, AI feels threatening; in context, it often looks like a gift.
If the public’s pessimism can be eased with specificity, employers have a tougher challenge. Short-term profitability concerns can push firms toward automation—eliminating jobs to capture immediate efficiency gains. But as Wharton professor Ethan Mollick has pointed out, that logic is self-defeating. If workers believe that showing AI-driven efficiency could cost them their jobs, they’ll have every reason to drag their feet, slowing the very efficiency companies are trying to achieve.
Research from MIT’s Task Force on the Work of the Future reinforces this point: automation may boost efficiency in the short run, but its benefits plateau when machines substitute for humans instead of complementing them leading to slower long-term growth and diminished institutional resilience. Conversely, augmented teams are better equipped to respond to shifting markets, innovate new products, and maintain customer trust.
Like electricity or computing, AI can multiply what people are capable of doing—if firms redesign work processes to blend human and AI strengths rather than settling for the evanescent financial benefits of reducing headcounts. Many business leaders appear to be understand this: a recent survey found that far more senior business leaders expect AI to increase, not decrease, hiring of interns and entry-level workers, which would stand recent concerns over employment of young grads on its head.
We live in an anxious age. But the evidence suggests that when it comes to AI that fear is rooted in a misunderstanding. When people see how AI improves rather than erases their work, support rises dramatically. The same principle applies to companies: those that use AI to empower rather than replace their employees will have the best chance to grow and thrive.
