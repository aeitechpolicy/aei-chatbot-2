	What Happens in Silicon Valley Doesn’t Stay There | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Tech-sector human resources policies tend to follow a familiar path: dismissal, ridicule, resistance, and eventual acceptance, often summarized as “first they dismiss you, then they ridicule you, then they fight you, and then you win.” When Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke announced a radical restructuring of hiring and performance management last April, much of Silicon Valley moved briskly through those stages. And, as Nate Jones documented on AI News & Strategy Daily, many who initially dismissed Lütke’s views are now quietly getting on board.
In April 2025, Lütke directed Shopify employees to treat AI use as a baseline expectation rather than a discretionary aid, integrating AI fluency directly into everyday workflows and performance reviews. He also banned hiring new staff until managers demonstrated the work could not be automated. Lütke’s memo framed AI not as a cost-cutting device, but as a capability multiplier—necessary for individual and organizational competitiveness.
What happens in Silicon Valley doesn’t stay there. Shopify’s policy was not merely a productivity push for a single firm; it was a signal of how AI is reshaping the talent market across the knowledge economy. What looks like a tech-sector quirk today is likely to become standard practice across much of the US economy, especially in knowledge-intensive sectors. That makes it worth examining the processes that drive this diffusion and considering their implications for education, training, and skill development.
In Reshuffle: Who Wins When AI Restacks the Knowledge Economy?, Sangeet Choudary argues that general-purpose technologies like AI diffuse not because they are novel, but because they drive changes in competitive ecosystems. First movers gain advantages in the form of higher productivity, better products, lower prices, and higher margins. Competitors are then forced to restructure or exit. Selection effects are not confined to biology; they are embedded in markets as well.
As a self-compounding technology, AI intensifies this process. Each round of deployment generates data. That data improves tools and systems. Better systems produce better outputs, which generate more data, further tightening the flywheel. Under these conditions, change is not merely continuous—it accelerates. Reinvention becomes the prime directive, and stability transforms into a liability.
Choudary goes on to note that these emergent competitive pressures—rather than the technology itself—are what ultimately reshape jobs and skills. Much of our (largely unsuccessful) effort to forecast the “skills of the future” fails because in-demand skills are increasingly ephemeral: here today, absorbed into automated systems tomorrow. At the same time, knowledge abundance and skill commodification create new forms of scarcity within the economy relating to the need to integrate, prioritize, verify, and decide what “good” looks like. This is why we should expect growth in coordination and synthesis roles centered on curation, judgment, and responsibility in AI-augmented teams.
As I often say to those who are concerned about the future of work, building a chip fabrication plant is simple compared to building adaptation and judgment inside a human being. Human tacit capacities develop gradually through social experiences, and are heavily shaped by family structure, social capital, and institutional quality. Human capability of this kind is not, strictly speaking, taught; it emerges from a matrix of healthy communities and functioning institutions. If you want to glimpse the future of workforce challenges, spend time in a kindergarten serving children from socioeconomically deprived families. Children struggling with executive functioning and negative externalizing often turn into young adults with impaired learning and adaptation skills.
The punchline of the Lütke memo—and the warning it carries for current and future workers—is headcount. Long before April 2025, Shopify had already adopted many of the practices the memo formalized; AI fluency and usage were already functioning as a human-resources sorting device. The company’s headcount peaked at 11,600 in 2022. By 2024, it had fallen to roughly 8,100, with subsequent rounds of layoffs reducing staff further. This does not mean Shopify is no longer talent-hungry. On the contrary, it has expanded its internship program from 75 to roughly 1,000 positions, using it both as a mechanism for identifying highly adaptable talent and as a launchpad for those not hired by Spotify to gain experience and credentials that are recognized across the market.
What Lütke ultimately named is the Red Queen reality of the AI-driven knowledge economy. When competition accelerates, improvement is the price of survival requiring everyone to move as fast as they can just to stay in place. AI makes that logic unavoidable by rapidly converting yesterday’s edge into today’s baseline, and today’s baseline into tomorrow’s sub-optimal. The question is no longer whether workers and firms can keep up, but whether we are building the institutional and human capacity to stay a step ahead of change.
