	Our Labor Force Demographics Are Getting Worse | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Just over a year ago, the labor market forecasting firm Lightcast released estimates of US labor supply trends with a straightforward warning: The United States was heading toward a demographics-driven labor shortage. Its one-year update is darker.
Lightcast economists Ron Hetrick and Hannah Grieser find that the trends they identified last year—Baby Boomer retirements, smaller “replacement” generations, and declining labor force participation—continue sloping down. Immigration has also slowed sharply. More importantly, these trends are now converging in ways that could quicken their constraints growth, hiring, and service delivery.
New data through 2024 show a modest rebound in marriage and birth rates after hitting historic lows in 2022–2023. But much of that bounce appears tied to the temporary surge in immigration between 2022 and early 2024. Foreign-born populations tend to have higher marriage and fertility rates than the native-born population. However, it still takes roughly twenty years to make a twenty-year-old. As Hetrick and Grieser note, whatever lift the country gets from immigrant fertility it won’t be felt for at least 10 years.
Whatever the bump from immigrant marriages and birth, labor force participation continues to slide. The participation rate fell from 62.7 percent in late 2024 to 62.4 percent by the end of 2025, a decline that accounts for hundreds of thousands of missing workers. This comes on top of roughly eight million additional people who remain outside the labor force relative to pre-pandemic levels, driven by accelerated retirements, softer economic conditions, and the withdrawal of foreign-born workers who face greater scrutiny during the immigration crackdown.
The exit of older workers is particularly consequential. Earlier forecasts assumed rising participation among older adults would offset slower growth among prime-age workers. The updated data say otherwise. The decline in the over-55 workforce is now both sharp and durable. By 2030, every Baby Boomer will be at least 66 years old, and apart from a sudden leap in life expectancy, there is little reason to expect a meaningful rebound in older-worker participation rates.
Even more sobering is the broader demographic picture. Updated projections indicate that the native-born US population will begin shrinking as early as 2031, eight years sooner than expected just a year ago. Because labor forces typically contract before populations do, this raises the possibility that the native-born labor force is already in decline, even if current survey data have not yet fully caught up.
According to Hetrick and Grieser, this means that projected labor force growth through 2034 has fallen from 6.4 million workers to around 3.5 million. Nearly all of the downward revision is tied to smaller projected prime-age populations, not to prime-age workers leaving the labor force at higher rates. The result is an economy with fewer producers and more consumers and a lot less slack than policymakers and employers have assumed.
Can artificial-intelligence-driven automation make up the difference? As Hetrick has argued elsewhere—most directly in his AEI conversation, “We Need People. Period.”—AI is unlikely to provide an escape hatch from demographic reality. He and Grieser show that AI adoption is advancing slowest in sectors like healthcare, construction, manufacturing, and logistics, fields that depend heavily on physical work and remain relatively resistant to the effects of generative AI. Even where automation is technically feasible, deployment is slowed by capital costs, regulation, and skill shortages.
Fewer working-age adults, lower participation, and fewer foreign born workers mean the United States will have to work harder to stretch its labor supply by reconfiguring jobs and accelerating technologies that make up for missing people. Revisiting our absurdly counterproductive immigration policies might also be helpful.
