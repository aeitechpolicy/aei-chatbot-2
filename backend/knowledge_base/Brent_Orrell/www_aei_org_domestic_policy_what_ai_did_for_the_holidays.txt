	What AI Did for the Holidays | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Across hardware, robotics, and reasoning, AI companies and systems made a series of consequential moves during the holiday break. Taken together, they point to an important shift: Advances in large language models are no longer the primary constraint on AI progress and deployment. Instead, the bottleneck is increasingly how those capabilities are embedded in hardware, workflows, and production systems. The result is not a sudden leap in intelligence, but the first steps toward AI becoming always-on infrastructure for business.
Nvidia’s $20 Billion Bet on Groq
Nvidia announced a roughly $20 billion deal with Groq (not that one, the other one), centered on access to Groq’s inference-chip technology and engineering talent. Both companies insist—rather too forcefully—that this is not a straight-up acquisition. The scientists and leadership moves to Nvidia, which also receives non-exclusive technology licensing. Groq, or what remains of it, continues as an independent entity.
Groq builds Language Processing Units that deliver faster AI responses while consuming less energy than general-purpose GPUs. That distinction matters in real-life scenarios, where delays of even a few milliseconds can slow customer-service systems, confuse robots, or add unnecessary strain to electrical grids.
The arrangement shows Nvidia’s push beyond selling raw compute to molding how AI performs within operational constraints, such as call centers, warehouses, factories, and other settings where systems must act in real time rather than simply generate answers. With model capability largely a given, these settings benefit most from low latency, predictability, and energy efficiency. As a result, manufacturing—where AI has previously had limited success—is beginning to move from experimental pilots toward early versions of smart factories with more agile and adaptive robotics.
The Rise of Work-and-Learn Robots
Two strands of recent research that are often discussed separately are converging on the same bottleneck: Robots that can adapt through continuous learning and understand basic physics. On the robotics side, work on closed-loop “Think, Act, Learn” (TAL) systems helps overcome the limitations of open-loop programming, which relies on comprehensively anticipating and planning each robotic action in dynamic environments. With open loops, when something goes wrong—an object slips, a surface shifts, or a human intervenes—the system is unable to adapt.
TAL close that loop. Perception, action, and learning remain in continuous dialogue. Execution is no longer the end of reasoning; it becomes part of it. If a robot drops an item, it stops, reassesses, and adjusts. This may sound incremental, but it marks a shift from choreography to improvisation.
Causal World Model Induction (CWMI) complements TAL through encoding an understanding of basic physical relationships like force, object persistence, and cause and effect, so that systems can anticipate what is likely to happen in unfamiliar situations. TAL supplies feedback, whereas CWMI helps guide action. Together, they move machines closer to becoming competent actors in the real world.
AI-Infused Manufacturing Pilots
These advances in cognition and robotics are now being tested in factories. At the Consumer Electronics Show last week, Siemens CEO Roland Busch announced a digital-twin platform built on Nvidia’s Omniverse that creates physics-accurate replicas of entire facilities. Fed by live production data, these twins allow engineers to simulate changes, anticipate failures, and adjust workflows before new facilities are built. The result is earlier problem detection, reduced downtime, and lower redesign costs—in short, greater and more efficient bang for the buck on capital investment.
Nvidia is also working with Caterpillar to embed AI directly into construction machinery. The Cat AI Assistant provides operators with on-demand access to safety and operational guidance while streaming roughly 2,000 messages per second to corporate servers with performance and operation data. Over time, this data is being used to build digital twins for future projects, showing how continuous data generation, collection, and analysis can compound process improvements. It is the wind-up to exponential improvement in how factories work.
What It Means
Individually, these gains are incremental. As they, and similar changes, spread across the economy, AI-enabled, self-compounding process improvements could deliver the productivity gains many have been anticipating. At the same time, sector readiness, deployment timelines, capital constraints, and the pace of workforce adjustment remain unresolved constraints on AI adoption. The labor-market effects of AI deployment, for good and ill, are unlikely to arrive as a single employment shock. Instead, they will emerge unevenly, as technological and marketplace pressures reshape skill demands, career ladders, and the intensity of work.
