	Can a New Methodology Give Us “Headlights” on AI Work Disruption? | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

A new working paper by Philip Moreira Tomei and Bouke Klein Teeselink, posted to arXiv in early May, makes claims that, if correct, should reorient the workforce policy conversation. In short, the authors argue that the AI exposure indices that have shaped most current thinking are looking at an incomplete subset of digital work. They identify which jobs and tasks current language models can already accelerate but they miss the jobs with features that make them amenable to automation later.
Tomei and Klein Teeselink build a new index that scores all 17,951 task statements in the federal O*NET database. The authors propose a measure what they call “reinforcement learning feasibility” which asks whether a task has the structural features (e.g., verifiable outcomes, use environments amenable to simulation, discrete decision/feedback loops) that allow AI systems to be trained on it through the post-training methods that are becoming the main drivers of AI capability. They then compare their index to the most-cited existing measure, from Eloundou and colleagues, which looks at whether tasks can be automated with current technology.
Across 894 occupations, the two indices agree closely. Bracket the 41 percent of tasks that require a physical body, however, and they diverge sharply. The Eloundou measure highlights the exposure of writers, lawyers, analysts, software developers, all text-centric knowledge work where large language models help with drafting and analysis. Tomei and Klein Teeselink highlight a different set: railroad conductors, power plant operators, aircraft cargo handling supervisors, gas plant operators. This “monitoring-and-control” work is not text-heavy but has the structural characteristics that reinforcement learning training requires. Using only the Eloudou taxonomy, these jobs are assumed to be AI-insulated.
The Tomei and Klein Teeselink paper is a preprint and the index rests on AI-generated ratings rather than human-validated ones. Time and peer review will tell us more about its usefulness.
In December 2024, Suzette Kent, Jason Owen-Smith, and I argued federal-state labor market information systems were not up to the task of generating useful AI forecasts. We called the existing system “rearview,” built to tell us what happened, sometimes years ago, at a generalized national level. What policymakers and workers need, we said, is “headlight” data that is regionally tuned to tell us where work is going. The Tomei and Klein Teeselink paper is a partial answer to this challenge. The properties that lead to automation are observable now; the displacement they point toward lags behind knowledge economy impacts.
The policy implication is not that we have been worrying about the wrong workers. The knowledge-economy exposure is real and immediate. My own recent work has focused on it because the displacement risk for white-collar workers is obvious and rising, and because the retraining approaches, as well as the programs for delivering them, are insufficient if the scale of the displacement turns out to be large.
Tomei and Klein Teeselink’s insights exacerbate the transition problem by adding a second category of work. Many monitoring-and-control occupations are concentrated in rural and exurban communities where retraining infrastructure and alternative employment are even thinner than in metropolitan areas. A displaced power plant operator in central Pennsylvania or a railroad conductor in rural Nevada faces a different problem than a displaced paralegal in Chicago: not just what to retrain for, but where the next job actually is. For regions that suffered greatly during the last round of automation impacts, this is a sobering prospect.
This is what the headlight metaphor was meant to surface. Forward-looking data on where AI is going is not a curiosity. It provides policymakers with the scope and depth to analyze the true nature of the problem we may confront and begin planning for it now.
