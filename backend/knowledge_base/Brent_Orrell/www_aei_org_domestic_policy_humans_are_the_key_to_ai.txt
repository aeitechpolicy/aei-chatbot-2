	Humans Are the Key to AI | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Sal Khan, founder of Khan Academy, made an admission recently that has implications for classrooms, cubicles, and wherever else generative AI is deployed. Speaking to Chalkbeat about Khanmigo, the AI tutor his organization built with OpenAI, Khan acknowledged, “For a lot of students, it was a non-event. They just didn’t use it much.”
That ought to make your ears perk up. Khan Academy is a best-case scenario for AI in the classroom: a mission-driven nonprofit with 190 million registered users, many of them motivated learners and families. Khan Academy’s chief learning officer, Kristen DiCerbo, told LinkedIn followers in May that only about 15 percent of students with access to Khanmigo regularly use it. The organization is now redesigning the product to prompt students rather than wait to be asked.
Given its history of over-promising and under-delivering, ed tech faces a high, well-deserved wall of skepticism. With AI, though, we should be careful about a rush to judgment. Some experiments are delivering real results.
Compare Khanmigo to two randomized controlled trials of technology-assisted instruction that did produce significant learning gains. In Nigeria’s Edo State, the World Bank tested Microsoft Copilot with 800 first-year secondary students over six weeks in mid-2024. An AI-fluent teacher led twice-weekly AI sessions, coaching students on how to use the tool effectively. The study documented student learning gains of 1.5 to 2 years, driven by the program’s structured design, curriculum alignment, and teacher support.
An earlier Mindspark trial split each 90-minute adaptive software tutoring session between self-driven software and small-group instruction. The trial produced significant gains in Hindi, and the authors could not separate tool and instruction effects. The decision not to test the components separately had the unexpected benefit of highlighting the importance of human and machine aspects of instruction. As Khan put it, the absence of a human intermediary is like sitting at the back of a class and waiting for students to seek help: “Some will; most won’t.”
A 2025 McKinsey survey revealed a similar dynamic in workplaces. High-performing organizations were about three times more likely to have reworked workflows around the technology. A recent New York Fed survey found that only 16 percent of workers say their employer offers AI training. Where tools arrive without the training to use them, the automation floats on the surface.
These dual conditions should encourage anyone worried about an AI jobs apocalypse. A working relationship between people and machines will take time and resources, which should slow displacement as firms learn to blend the two. This is the human “wrapper” function – technology mediated through human beings – and developing it requires experience, mentorship, and practice.
AI accelerationists and enthusiasts seem to assume everyone shares their agency, energy, and innate skill for technological novelty when, in reality, few people are like the tech natives. They don’t live for every new innovation. Most have limited experience with computers beyond Google searches. They will need help: education, training, and, frankly, hand-holding, if they, and we, are to capture AI’s benefits.
Failure, acknowledged and accepted, becomes learning. Khan Academy is learning, like the millions of other businesses, organizations, and individuals, taking their first steps toward forging useful human-AI partnerships. Trial and correction are the way of technological adaptation. Whether generative AI works will depend less on the models than on the human systems built around them.
