	Teaching Machines to Remember | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

You may not have heard about it, but the Bay Area was rattled late last week. Not by an earthquake, but by a new development in large-language-model (LLM) architecture that could set artificial intelligence on a more human-like course.
Researchers at Google have released a paper describing efforts to align LLM learning and memory more closely with how the human brain works. The work builds on the insights that earned Geoffrey Hinton the 2024 Nobel Prize for modeling artificial neural networks on biological processes—the foundational breakthrough behind today’s AI revolution.
Existing LLMs, however, suffer from a significant limitation: amnesia. Each time a model is retrained, it treats the new information as disconnected from what it already knows. In AI-world, this flaw is known as “catastrophic forgetting.” To work around it, engineers have built ever-larger systems—billions of parameters deep—that consume staggering amounts of computing power and electricity.
Google’s new research aims to overcome this cyber absent-mindedness with a concept called “nested learning” (NL). Extending Hinton’s brain-as-model concept, NL introduces layers of memory that update at different speeds. Short-term layers adapt quickly; intermediate ones consolidate patterns; and long-term layers change slowly to reflect durable changes. This mimics how human learning works: knowledge accumulates, stabilizing over time while remaining plastic—capable of integrating new information.
For now, NL exists on paper and in controlled lab experiments as a proof-of-concept network called HOPE. But the implications could be substantial. NL appears to be an element of recursive AI, in which algorithms evolve and refine their own learning over time. It could also enhance machines’ ability to generalize—applying lessons from prior training to new contexts, a major step forward for fields like robotics. In the long run, nested learning might also improve AI efficiency by reducing the need for frequent and expensive retraining cycles. And all that integrated learning and knowledge would be critical to AI-led innovation.
If it proves scalable, NL may mark a turning point in AI’s evolution—from systems that store and regurgitate information to ones that learn, remember, and adapt more like we do. The road from lab experiments to everyday applications will likely be long, but Google’s work hints at a future where artificial intelligence doesn’t just get bigger—it gets smarter, more predictable, and more human in the way it learns.
