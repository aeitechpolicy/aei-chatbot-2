	AI Will Test Rural Firms and Workers | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

As we closed out 2025, much of the concern about artificial intelligence (AI) and the future of work focused on whether AI automation was reducing employment opportunities for college graduates seeking to join the knowledge economy. The best empirical evidence said “probably,” either directly (through task automation) or indirectly as knowledge sector employers slowed hiring in anticipation of future automation.
A new paper in Nature Communications shifts attention to small and mid-sized firms in rural America. Analyzing more than 160 million job ads, the authors find that lower-wage, lower-skill occupations are undergoing the most significant skill demand changes. Because these jobs are disproportionately found in small and mid-sized businesses and rural regions, the burden of transition falls hardest on firms with fewer resources and on workers who have less access to education and training needed for AI-enabled work.
Firms in metro areas tend to operate closer to the technological frontier, reducingthe “distance” between the old skills and new and allowing for more incremental change. Denser labor markets means workers are better able to change employers without changing occupations. Metropolitan firms tend to have more internal training capacity and workers a more robust education and training ecosystem. Rural labor markets look very different: smaller firms, more limited occupational options, and truncated job ladders. New technology often requires larger leaps rather than gradual steps.
The new evidence suggests that rural jobs are not simply adding a few new tools. They are undergoing deeper reconfiguration. Workers are being asked to move from task execution to systems management and supervision roles that are less related to what they were previously hired to do, increasing strain on both workers and employers.
The new, higher level skills are more difficult to teach, typically requiring long-term, apprenticeship-like training that involves mentorship, iterative try-fail-try-again experiences, and exposure to handling exceptions to general work rules. AI-enabled systems may improve efficiency and productivity, but they also require workers who can interpret outputs, manage failures, and integrate technology into real-world operations. Companies in urban areas will have to take significant steps to reconfigure work and skills. In nonmetropolitan areas it’s more like leaps but against a backdrop of weaker education and training systems. This mismatch between the speed, depth, and breadth of change and the relative weakness of local institutions helps account for the gap between metro tech optimism and rural foreboding.
If this sounds familiar, there’s a good reason. If this study is correct, it points to a possible automation shock similar to the one experienced in the 1990s and early 2000s. While we don’t know and can’t forecast specific changes to jobs and skills, we ought to begin thinking through public policy design questions for responding to another transition that may disproportionately fall on non-metro America. These options might include expanding work-based learning in production, maintenance, and supervisory roles; strengthening community college capacity for sector-focused simulation and applied training; and reconsidering the tax treatment of business investment in human capital development.
For large metropolitan firms, AI-driven change often presents a problem of complexity—how to reorganize work at the margin, reallocate tasks, and integrate new tools into already dense organizational and labor-market ecosystems. For small firms in rural labor markets, AI the challenge is more about a sharp break in firm organization and worker skills under conditions of education and training scarcity. Where institutional supports are weakest, the risks of disruption are greatest—and so too is the case for deliberate, contextualized strategies that help rural firms and workers navigate change rather than bear its costs alone.
