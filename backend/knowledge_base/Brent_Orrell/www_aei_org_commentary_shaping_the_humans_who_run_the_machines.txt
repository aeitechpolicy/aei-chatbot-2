	Shaping the Humans Who Run the Machines | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Spencer Klavan’s lead forum essay Law & Liberty essay, “AI in an Age of Humanity,” makes a claim that deserves much more attention than it is getting. While we obsess over imminent job automation, universal basic incomes, and a Terminator-style apocalypse, we are skipping past the more proximate problems and opportunities that could make us participants in the future rather than mere spectators.
Klavan’s argument is practical and philosophically moored. Artificial intelligence, far from being transcendent, is derivative. It recombines human-generated input at scales we could never reach unassisted. AI is just us, all that we have ever written, said, or done, accelerated beyond comprehension. Originality is not—yet—one of its strengths. Train a model on its own output, Klavan notes, and it starts to come undone.
A counterintuitive lesson follows. If you want a place in our AI-driven future, become the best human being you possibly can: one who thinks deeply, searches for meaning, and refuses recycled, superficial knowledge. AI can supplant us only if we, by drift and indifference, allow it to. For now, we are still in charge, and we should act like it.
The philosophical claim is right. The institutional implications go further than Klavan takes them.
Where the Philosophy Meets Data
Empirical evidence supports his insights. The OECD reported in 2024 that the skills most in demand in AI-exposed occupations are not technical but managerial: weighing competing priorities and exercising practical judgment. Research by the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas finds that experience premiums are rising in AI-exposed occupations, not falling. Information sector output has roughly doubled since 2006, with working hours flat, and the sector’s productivity growth has remained strong since 2019, tracking the diffusion of AI tools into knowledge work. Taken together, the evidence suggests that AI rewards managers, experienced workers, and, by extension, the institutions that produce them. The bulk of AI’s value depends upon the human capacity to guide and mediate it.
The standard account is that AI works best with a “human in the loop.” This phrase emerges from minds deeply shaped by technology: the tech is the main thing, and the human is an occasionally useful add-on, the quality controller and manager of the machine-produced conclusion. This formulation has the relationship backwards.
The biggest problem with AI, as many have noted, is that it does not “get it.” Its utility collapses around questions of continuity, and intellectual and social context. And “getting it,” as we have known all along, is the most important aspect of life and work.
Consider Klavan’s oncologist example. The “in the loop” account treats the doctor as quality control: the model produces a finding, the physician verifies it, and a standardized treatment plan follows. This is a massive truncation of what oncologists actually do. They read scans in the context of the patient’s history, family situation, stage of life, the benefits and risks of treatments, and personal values, and then communicate recommendations in a compassionate manner to frightened patients and families. Strip away technology and medical care is revealed as an inherently humanistic project. Identifying the malady is one thing. Knowing what to do about it, and aligning treatment with the patient and a wider set of concerns and values, is another. Without those inputs, the imaging report is a claxon in a cave.

The same deficiency shows up wherever AI is now useful. A policy analyst working with an AI-generated brief is not fact-checking. The work begins in deciding which problems matter, weighing contradictory data and arguments, fashioning solutions that must reconcile with moral and ideological priors, and then persuading others to adopt them. In each case, the model produces material, and the human decides on questions, meaning, and use. Humans and their values are not the backstop. They are the alpha and omega of the exercise.
This is the operational form of Klavan’s observation that “we are the juice.” It is also why a model trained on its own output dissolves. The collapse is not a quirk of predictive analytics. It is what happens when you strip out the human layers that supply real-time context and intuition. AI tends to raise the burden of diagnosis, design, and praxis rather than lightening it.
The Institutional Question
If human judgment supplies meaning on the front end and context on the back, the practical question is which institutions produce humans capable of that delicate, nuanced work. Klavan’s essay points toward this issue without fully engaging it.
Judgment is a metacognitive capacity formed over time, through exposure to cases, contexts, ethical stakes, and trial-and-error learning. That is a journey we cannot start, much less complete, on our own. It requires institutions explicitly designed to form human beings into judgment-bearing persons.
As things currently stand, America’s institutions of formation are unprepared for the AI future. Families are stressed. Religious and civic infrastructure has been shrinking and ossifying for decades. Schools are flooded with mandates and standardized tests, and captive to a credentialing culture that sees education mainly as preparation for work, narrowly conceived as the acquisition of market-relevant skills. Under this regime, someone who has completed the coursework and passed the examinations is presumed able to execute a given task. Whether they are ready to grapple with ambiguity and make decisions under uncertainty are different questions, and ones we rarely test or even have a clear idea of how to assess.
The credentialing system was built on the assumption that codified knowledge was the scarce input and judgment was plentiful. Under AI, this priority reverses. Codified knowledge is now abundant, generated and recombined at a scale and speed no individual can match. Judgment has become the scarce input. The judgment deficit among American workers, a fact doubters can verify by spending an afternoon with some employers, is the inadvertent product of decades of design choices that elevated narrow technique over broad context. The implication is not that credentials should be abolished or that technical knowledge does not matter. Even in an AI-driven economy that rapidly automates the skills we are credentialing, technical knowledge remains indispensable to governing AI systems. The point is that credentials are only part of what success in career and life requires. Judgment is always useful in day-to-day decisions. In our AI-driven future, it may also be a sine qua non of economic participation and professional growth.
Liberal education at its best does this work through case-based learning, Socratic inquiry, and exposure to traditions of moral reasoning. So does serious career and technical education that puts learners in real work environments under real stakes. Rigorous sector-based training is another approach that builds durable skills alongside technical ones. Apprenticeships, residencies, and the older professional formation traditions are also successful when they combine supervised practice with structured investigation and reflection.
These are not separate tracks competing for funding and prestige. They are different routes to the same end: the formation of judgment under conditions where both questions and answers defy codification.
The Risk of a Small Humanity
Without deliberate institutional design, humanity’s already limited capacity to shape AI will thin out. A few firms and leaders, having invested early, will control their development and use, with the help of a relative handful of workers. We can hope that those at the top have been adequately formed to help all of us flourish. As Scottish jurors used to say, “Not proven.” A society capable of retaining and protecting humanness will need a much broader base of people at least somewhat acquainted with the ideas of human uniqueness and value and prepared to know when those characteristics are being encroached upon.
No single institution can own responsibility for formation. Family, religion, civic association, education, and business each look to the others to lead, and none can do it alone. The result is a tragedy of the skills commons: a shared resource of human judgment we all draw from, but few tend. Expecting technologists to take up the banner is theoretically nice, but historically unsupported. From the digital and social media historical record, we have little reason to expect much beyond technological optimization from industry. What is required is a renewed, broad-based commitment to the institutions of human formation with the explicit intent of building our capacity to meet the technological moment. We are, as Klavan says, in charge. But only if we summon the courage, energy, and self-belief to own the challenge.
