	When AI Starts Reading the Room | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Mira Murati’s Thinking Machines Lab announced this week that it is building “interaction models,” a new class of AI designed to process audio, video, and text simultaneously and respond at conversational speed. The initial system, TML-Interaction-Small, reportedly clocks response latency at roughly 0.4 seconds, close to the timing of natural human conversation and faster than comparable models from OpenAI and Google. A limited research preview is expected within months, with broader release later in 2026.
Today’s leading AI systems, in their default text and voice modes, operate in a single thread. The human types, the model waits, and then the model responds. Real human conversation does not work that way. We interrupt, hesitate, adjust mid-sentence, and read each other’s faces and tone before either party finishes a thought. Albert Mehrabian’s research established that when meaning is ambiguous or emotionally weighted, listeners draw heavily on facial expression and vocal tone, not just words. AI systems have not, thus far, been able to decode these unspoken signals.
This is where Murati’s work comes in. Interaction models aim to smooth the human-machine exchange. The technical approach processes audio and visual signals directly with the result that an AI can register a gesture or a hesitation and adjust before the sentence finishes.
Anthropic has been working on a related problem from a different angle. Claude Code recently added a /btw command that lets a user insert a side question while the system is processing the main task, without interrupting the work in progress. It is a useful workflow feature, but it is not the same thing because it still involves significant latency. Thinking Machines is pursuing a harder version of the problem: not parallel channels of communication, but a single channel of communication that runs in both directions simultaneously.
If the approach scales, it could significantly alter how humans and AI relate, moving from a volley-return approach to a more fluid system that mimics how human-to-human communication works. Two people talking are not taking turns producing outputs. They are reading each other in real time and adjusting tone and content accordingly, calibrating against an internalized sense of whether participants are in agreement or not.
Adam Smith said this process of mutual exchange is what creates the impartial spectator, the imagined observer whose judgment we rehearse against as we speak and act. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith argued that this faculty is what forms and informs mutual understanding and shapes our sense of propriety. In more contemporary terms, it is how we develop a “theory of mind” about what others are thinking, enabling anticipatory exchange. The point is not that AI interaction models possess an impartial spectator or the means of acquiring one. Rather, it is that such models, by measuring what’s said as well as what isn’t, move us closer to something like real human-machine conversation.
Ray Kurzweil has long argued that the boundary between human and machine cognition will become less distinct over time as the two converge. Setting aside Kurzweil’s timeline and his transhumanist conclusions, interaction models are a step toward this convergence. The human-machine border remains real but the exchange boundary between human and machine becomes less distinct.
Interaction models also heighten risks associated with one of AI’s best documented problems: sycophancy, the tendency to praise user genius and tell them what they want to hear. Models trained on human feedback learn that agreement and validation produce better ratings, a feedback loop that drives AI toward agreement with faulty human assumptions. Interaction models may create an even starker version of this problem. A system reading subtle facial and verbal cues has a new reinforcement goal to chase, and a feedback loop that’s essentially instantaneous.
Assuming that Thinking Machines can make this work (which I do), it could be the leading edge of a much more significant change in how humans and AI work together. This raises a very hard question: How will we adapt once social latency disappears and the machine starts reading the room?
