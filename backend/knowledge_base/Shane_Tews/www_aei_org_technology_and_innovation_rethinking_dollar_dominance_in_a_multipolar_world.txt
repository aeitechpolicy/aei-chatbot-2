	Rethinking Dollar Dominance in a Multipolar World | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

As the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT) and the US dollar become increasingly leveraged in geopolitical disputes, the broader BRICS coalition (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa) and the Sino-Russian partnership have accelerated efforts to construct parallel financial systems, ranging from China’s Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) network to new blockchain-based tools like BRICS Pay. For the United States, this shift threatens the dollar’s central role and creates new, less transparent channels for cross-border transactions, eroding the intelligence advantages once provided by SWIFT and altering the balance of financial power worldwide.
You will find the highlights of my conversation with Milton Mueller, a professor at the Georgia Tech School of Public Policy and co-founder of the Internet Governance Project; Karim Farhat, assistant director of the Internet Governance Project; Vagisha Srivastava, a PhD researcher at Georgia Tech specializing in digital currency and cybersecurity; and Nicoletta Kolpakov, director of the Cirrus Institute, a research organization that focuses on emerging technologies. Their expertise offers a comprehensive view of how digital currencies, stablecoins, and national payment systems are reshaping monetary sovereignty. Our conversation shed light on a world where payment infrastructures, digital identity, and geopolitical strategy are becoming increasingly intertwined.
Below is a lightly edited and abridged transcript of our discussion. You can listen to this and other episodes of Explain to Shane on AEI.org and subscribe via your preferred listening platform. If you enjoyed this episode, leave us a review, and tell your friends and colleagues to tune in.
Shane Tews: How did US sanctions end up strengthening China and Russia’s position? The intent of restricting SWIFT access was to limit their international banking capabilities, not to encourage the development of an alternative system.
Karim Farhat: Yeah, sure. I mean, the Chinese have been building their own payment rails for a while now—we’ve seen that with CIPS. But I think what the US government perhaps inadvertently did was force their hand with the sanctions on Russia by confiscating Russian treasuries.
That sent a very clear signal to the rest of the non-aligned countries that the money they hold in US dollar-denominated assets could be confiscated. It’s not outside the realm of possibility. Now, of course, there is a reaction and a trade-off. Treasury has to carefully balance the risk of doing that against other countries beginning to de-dollarize, as you alluded to.
But what we find—and Vagisha can back me up on this—is that a lot of the threat, especially after COVID, tended to be overstated regarding the risk of global de-dollarization. We still see many transactions in dollars; I believe around 59 percent of foreign transactions are denominated in dollars right now in terms of trade.
So, there is still a lot of strength to the dollar as a global network, and that does not disappear overnight. But it is important to start thinking about what the US government should be doing—not taking a reactionary stance, but proactively considering what can be done in the future.
Nicoletta, in your paper you discuss the legal constructs we need to consider, including the quiet erosion of the dollar through the IMF. You also highlight how the legal system may need to adapt. What are your thoughts on how these legal frameworks should evolve?
Nicoletta Kolpakov: So, I think we are seeing a form of legal fragmentation as new payment systems emerge outside the traditional Western legal order. There is an intensification of what you could call jurisdictional divergence. As global finance moves toward regionalization, we’re seeing a corresponding fragmentation. One of the primary challenges in this shift is that divergence, particularly in forum selection and the enforcement of cross-border financial transactions.
Each nation or bloc participating in these alternative financial systems can apply its own set of rules for digital contracts, smart contracts, and financial transactions. In a blockchain-based financial system, the legal recognition of digital signatures and smart contracts remains inconsistent across jurisdictions. While some countries have embraced blockchain technology and related frameworks, others remain hesitant.
As a result, contract performance will vary, making holistic enforcement uncertain. Sanctions enforcement faces similar obstacles. The US and EU rely on tools like SWIFT and CHIPS, and these mechanisms lose power when financial systems become fragmented or decentralized. States like Russia and China, which develop alternatives, become increasingly resistant to Western sanctions, further undermining their effectiveness.
I still think the real test to Western dominance lies in the future of the US dollar, and hopefully decentralization can also create opportunities for governments to cooperate in that regard.
How is interoperability currently functioning, and how should it be structured going forward? In your paper, you argue that the right model could strengthen the US position—what is your recommendation?
Milton Mueller: I’d say there is a real tension between the desire in the United States to use monetary systems to control what other countries do and the market forces that make a currency network acceptable and widely used.
To the extent that you try to enhance control, you actually undermine the acceptability—or what Austrian economists call the salability—of the currency. We are focused on enhancing salability and market forces.
We want people, not governments, to control money. And we think the US has overreached a bit. There is a danger that this approach is encouraging alternative currencies backed by other geopolitical actors. The real win for the US, and for the public, is to strengthen tradability and market-driven exchanges internationally, rather than empowering one political authority over another.
What do you see as the tipping point that would normalize the digitization of finance? At what moment does digital money move from being used by early adopters to becoming a standard part of the global financial system, despite challenges like sovereign control, surveillance concerns, and friction between traditional banks and fintechs?
Vagisha Srivastava: I think four things need to work really well. First is the monetary aspect—people need trust in the underlying currency. And I don’t think trust in the dollar is declining as quickly as it is sometimes portrayed. It’s still there; people trust the dollar globally.
The second element is the payment rail. Who is designing these systems? Which countries are stepping in? What does the infrastructure look like? Is it private? Is it prone to surveillance? Is it safe for people to use? There are standards issues as well: whose standard ultimately wins? China’s? The US’s? BRICS’? Or even private actors like Visa and Mastercard, which are already partnering with various countries to help design their payment rails?
The third factor is the legal framework. As Nicolette pointed out earlier, what do bilateral swaps and country alignments look like? How will these arrangements support the functioning of the currency?
I think when all of these elements come together, that’s when we might see one model emerge. But I don’t think it will be easy. I would add that I recently ran a survey on digital currency adoption, and a key finding was that people are very willing to adopt it if there are government incentives. If it’s cheaper, more efficient, or helps them make more money, then many are willing to give up a bit of privacy. That could become the tipping point—although I hope it isn’t.
