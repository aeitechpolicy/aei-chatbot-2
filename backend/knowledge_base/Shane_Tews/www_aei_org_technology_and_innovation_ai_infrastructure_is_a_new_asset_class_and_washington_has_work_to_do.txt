	AI Infrastructure Is a New Asset Class, and Washington Has Work to Do. | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

For most of the past decade, Washington has treated broadband, electric power, and data infrastructure as separate policy problems. Separate agencies, separate hearings, and separate funding streams. That model is obsolete. AI has collapsed the distance between fiber, watts, and compute into a single integrated stack, and the capital markets figured it out before regulators did.
The numbers aren’t incremental. Goldman Sachs now projects approximately $7.6 trillion in cumulative AI infrastructure capital expenditure from 2026 to 2031, covering compute, data centers, and power. The five largest hyperscalers, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, and Oracle, will collectively spend between $90 billion and $100 billion in 2026, nearly doubling their 2025 investment. Morgan Stanley expects $250 billion to $300 billion in debt issuance in 2026 from hyperscalers and their joint ventures, and JPMorgan projects that annual data center securitization could reach $30 billion to $40 billion in both 2026 and 2027. When Wall Street securitizes data centers as bonds, it signals that the market has classified them as infrastructure investment rather than technology spending.
The binding constraint is now power, not code; and the most important shift in this asset class is that the limiting input is no longer silicon or software but electricity. A single AI training facility can require 100 to 500 megawatts of continuous power, comparable to the electricity demand of a small city. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) recognized this reality on June 18, when it approved its most significant grid interconnection reform in years, targeting a reduction in connection timelines from the current multi-year backlog to roughly 90 days.
That’s a meaningful signal from a federal regulator: The power interconnection process was not designed for the speed or scale of AI buildout. FERC can expedite the queue, but it cannot accelerate transmission permitting, site new generation, or resolve the multi-state coordination required to move electrons from where they’re produced to where they’re needed. The upstream bottleneck remains.
Goldman Sachs’ April 2026 update projects that global data center power demand will rise by more than 220 percent from 2023 levels, reaching a record 1,350 terawatt-hours by 2030. This is an upward revision from prior forecasts, driven by faster-than-anticipated AI server deployment. Gartner estimates that power shortages could constrain 40 percent of AI data centers by 2027. That’s not a catastrophe scenario. It’s a near-term operational risk built into current trajectories.
The new asset class question is not only whether infrastructure gets built, but also who builds it, under what obligations, and who bears the cost when private investment decisions create systemic externalities. The hyperscalers have made a rational choice: build around the shared system. Amazon, Microsoft, and Google are building private fiber, signing behind-the-meter power agreements, and designing dedicated cooling systems. From a purely business-logic standpoint, this makes sense; their revenue depends on the reliability that commodity infrastructure cannot guarantee at scale.
The policy challenge is what happens to the economics of shared infrastructure when the largest potential customers opt out. Requiring growth to pay for itself is a defensible goal, but achieving it depends less on presidential promises than on the careful application of just and reasonable principles by the institutions that regulate electricity.
The structural problem is asymmetric regulation: traditional carriers and utilities operate under must-serve obligations, while hyperscalers can invest or withdraw through private contracts without comparable public accountability. That imbalance will compound over time, and the policy community hasn’t kept pace. Fiber, watts, and compute are now a single investment category with a single set of physical constraints, yet the institutions governing them still operate as if they were unrelated.
During infrastructure booms, scarcity and time-to-power often matter more than brand visibility. Today, the scarcest input is not compute, it’s power availability and the political will to build the infrastructure needed to deliver it. If left unaddressed, that scarcity will concentrate AI’s economic benefits in corridors that already have adequate infrastructure.
The asset-class framing for consumers also has an equity dimension that warrants attention. Roger Entner identifies a causal loop: people with fiber connections run heavier AI workloads than those on DSL or fixed wireless. Connectivity quality does more than shape the user experience; it determines which households and communities can participate in AI’s economic gains.
The Administration and Congress can advance progress without creating new agencies or subsidy structures. They should reform the National Environmental Policy Act review process for grid infrastructure, including transmission lines and interconnections. Transmission permitting is the most time-consuming step in the energy stack and the area with the fewest policy levers. Bipartisan support exists in principle, but reform has repeatedly failed when bundled with unrelated issues.
Capital markets have classified AI infrastructure as a distinct asset class. The policy architecture that governs it still belongs to the last industrial era. That gap is the real bottleneck.
