	AI Cybersecurity Can’t Wait for Washington: Why Industry Must Lead | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Two events this month reveal something important about how artificial intelligence governance will work. The National Institute for Standards and Technology (NIST) Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) announced pre-deployment evaluation agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI. Anthropic quietly restructured its Project Glasswing cybersecurity program, overhauling how its Mythos model shares vulnerability findings with the outside world by allowing Project Glasswing partners to share AI cyber threat insights.
President Trump halting the planned executive order on AI shows that the government is taking AI oversight seriously. But the second development is more instructive because it illustrates a principle policymakers must internalize before building too much architecture on an incorrect assumption: in AI-driven cybersecurity, problems move faster than government can keep up, and solutions should come from industry first.
For decades, cybersecurity operated under a fragile yet functional social contract: obscurity offered a measure of protection, and the slow pace of vulnerability discovery gave defenders enough time to patch them before attackers could exploit them. AI has broken that bargain. Frontier models can now accelerate the identification of zero-day vulnerabilities to the point that the traditional “discover, disclose, patch” cycle is dangerously inadequate.
Anthropic’s Glasswing program was designed to address exactly this problem. Its Mythos model works with partners to identify and predict security weaknesses, providing the kind of AI-augmented threat intelligence the defensive community desperately needs. But the program launched under a strict confidentiality framework. Partners themselves requested the cone of silence, worried that disclosing AI-assisted findings might hand adversaries a roadmap.
By May, Anthropic concluded that the risks of keeping that intelligence siloed outweighed the risks of sharing it. The revised Mythos guidelines now authorize partners to share three vulnerability findings, security tools, and critical mitigation insights with regulators, open-source maintainers, and the public, consistent with standard responsible disclosure norms. The fix must travel with the flaw. That’s the right call. It took a frontier AI lab, not a federal agency, to make it happen.
The industry is working to set guidance amid real-time challenges. What Anthropic did with Glasswing is a case study in responsible AI deployment in practice, not a compliance exercise. It was a genuine operational response to a problem that the company’s own technology helped create. They built the capability, recognized the asymmetric risk it introduced, and restructured their disclosure framework to tip the balance back toward the defender. That sequence unfolded entirely within the industry, on the industry’s timeline. Governments should pay attention to this collaboration and encourage the information-sharing path over a government-first regulatory process.
This is not an argument against government engagement. CAISI’s pre-release evaluation framework is directionally correct by moving oversight upstream and is smarter than scrambling to respond after something goes wrong. Having frontier labs at the table gives the framework real credibility across diverse development models.
But there is a meaningful difference between government as an informed partner and government as the lead. In cybersecurity, the threat environment evolves in hours, not regulatory cycles. The Glasswing pivot happened because Anthropic had the technical context to recognize a shifting risk calculus and the operational agility to act on it. A federal agency running the same program would still be drafting its comments when the comment period closed.
A good government partnership is not government-led and industry-following. It is industry moving at the pace the problem demands, with government structured to absorb those updates, codify emerging standards, and fill the gaps industry cannot or will not address on its own. That requires real-time information channels. Federal evaluators need standing mechanisms to receive vulnerability intelligence from programs like Glasswing as it develops. Not through formal reporting cycles, but through continuous communication that matches the pace of AI-driven threat discovery. Standards should follow, not precede, practice. The safety benchmarks being developed through the Center’s pre-release agreements should be grounded in what industry has learned from programs like Glasswing, not imposed before operational experience.
Anthropic’s Glasswing pivot is the clearest recent example of what frontier AI safety looks like when it is operational rather than aspirational: a company identifying a risk created by its own technology, building a response, and restructuring how it shares intelligence to support the defensive ecosystem. This is the model.
Washington’s job is not to replicate that. It simply cannot move fast enough. Its role is to be a fast-follow partner: absorbing what the industry learns, setting minimum floors to prevent the worst outcomes, and ensuring that the companies best positioned to solve these problems are not the only ones allowed to compete. Industry must lead in AI cybersecurity. The government must be ready to keep up.
