So l'Il list a few, one at a time. Just give us your reaction as to what's going on and what the impacts might be. One is eliminating the requirement of a majority vote of the commissioners to approve the start of an investigation. Now, a single commissioner can sign off and launch an investigation. What are your thoughts on that?

[Speaker: Bilal Sayyed]
Bilal Sayyed: I have mixed thoughts on that. I mean, I think I have concerns about the commission structure as a five-member body. It doesn't always allow for speed in moving, and it also allows for conflicting messages either through the public or the staff. It also potentially allows for delays in investigation. However, the place was set up as a commission, and removing what could be a deliberative exchange between the commissioners before the start of an investigation could be harmful. I think a better approach would've been to try to impose some deadlines on the approval of such things. There are, under the commission's internal rules, some deadlines. But they're both often not adhered to and somewhat flexible. Speed is often a virtue (though not always), but I think this change loses some of the benefits of deliberation pre-investigation or before expanding the scope of an investigation. And in that sense, it was a mistake. There may have been a better way to address the concerns than just eliminating the input of four of the commissioners.

[Speaker: Mark Jamison]
Mark Jamison: Another procedural change we've heard about has been that the chairperson now has the power to issue subpoenas. What are your thoughts on that?

[Speaker: Bilal Sayyed]
Bilal Sayyed: Well, I think it's the same thing, right? There's some benefit to a model that looks a lot like the Department of Justice model, where you don't have to get a majority of votes to do things and you don't have delay that's caused by deliberation or back and forth among commissioners and the staff. One criticism of the agency — which may or may not be true when looked at in the past, but is probably something to be concerned about moving forward — was to ask: How quickly did the agency move to identify, investigate, and potentially remediate anti-competitive or anti-consumer activity? And so removing some of the hurdles to delay is a benefit. But again, it probably could have been done in a way that didn't place substantial power within one commissioner when the place was set up as a five-commissioner body, right? It seems inconsistent with having what is a bipartisan or multi-partisan commission to put authority into a chairman. Bill Kovacic has done some good work, as have others. But Bill in particular showed relative stability across the commission and across different presidential administrations — as compared to the Department of Justice, where you have a single head instead of an arguably collegial body. And I think moving to a model that looks more like the Antitrust Division loses some of the benefits of a bipartisan or multi-partisan body. There are inefficiencies associated with that body, but there are some benefits to it. And if what you want to see is head-snapping changes in policy across administrations, that's what they're setting up for. And that seems like a mistake. It seems like either it does politicize the agency or it looks like the agency's become politicized when the changing of one person (the chair) can make a big difference in the activity that the commission does.

[Speaker: Mark Jamison]
Mark Jamison: Something else that's expanded the role of the chairperson is eliminating the role of the agency's chief administrative law judge and overseeing fact-finding. Now that the chairperson will designate someone to be head of fact-finding procedures. What are your thoughts there?

[Speaker: Bilal Sayyed]
Bilal Sayyed: The role, the quality, and the strength of the administrative law judges — and the processes by which tacts are developed in a trial setting and then move up to the commission for appellate review — have been a concern of commissioners and the commission in the past — a dozen or so years ago, maybe a little bit longer. One of the commissioners effectively sat as an administrative law judge in a trial proceeding, and that had both benefits and costs. So again, I think there is a slowness to the agency's actions that should be addressed. Improvements to the case development and what I'II call the "judicial" role of the agency are, I think, appropriate. Though one criticism of the FTC is that when the commission votes to bring a case that's presented to them by the staff, then that case goes before either a federal court judge or an administrative court judge. If it's an administrative court judge, it's sort of within the FTC's system. The administrative law judge's decision is reviewed in the first instance by the commission. And so the people who voted to put a matter into litigation and into the administrative system often then get to vote and opine on whether the administrative law judge's

That looks problematic to me and makes you wonder about due process concerns. But clearly, there should be some changes in the process of bringing cases through the administrative law system. This one just doesn't seem like a good one to me, in theory. In practice, if she picks a special master or a retired, prominent antitrust person, well, maybe that would be good. First, it's hard for me to believe - whether it's management staff or other staff, whether they're making presentations at an ABA meeting or at a conference put on by a consumer group, a law school, or a trade association — that this has a significant effect, or even any effect, on the case process. In fact, I think it probably has a beneficial effect on generating cases, because when the staff is out there talking about the cases they're interested in and that they want to bring — as well as the harms and the concerns they have — I think you actually generate cases that way. Sol think it's a mistake to curtail this kind of outreach by the staff. It's not just lawyers going to conferences where they serve good food and you pal around with some guy who you might ask for a job in a year or two. I mean, this is real outreach to community organizations, and it does have the effect, I think, of generating cases. And so I think it's a mistake, even if the agency is resource- constrained, as they appear to be. And I think it's also consistent with what I call this false effort at greater transparency. The agency has started to have open meetings and to post petitions they receive. They post them for public comment, as if this was an adency that was not transparent in the past and did not seek public input in the past. That perception is just untrue. The agency, probably more than any other agency that I'm aware of, has the public input in its conferences and through these outreach opportunities by the commissioners and staff.
