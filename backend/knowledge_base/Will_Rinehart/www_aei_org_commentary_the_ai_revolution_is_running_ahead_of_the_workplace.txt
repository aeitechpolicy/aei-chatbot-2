	The AI Revolution Is Running Ahead of the Workplace | American Enterprise Institute - AEI

As a child, I was obsessed with my grandfather’s red tractor, a classic Farmall H. When it wasn’t being used, you could find it parked between the garden and the barn, like a sphinx protecting the corn fields. I’ve since learned that the Farmall was truly revolutionary for the farms and families where I grew up in Illinois.Tractors first spread through the Wheat Belt in the late 1910s, but it took another two decades for the technology to be adopted in the Corn Belt. By looking closely at Census data on tractors from 1910 to 1950, Daniel Gross of the Harvard Business School was able to plot out the two time periods. At first, the tractor was “developed for narrow applications with existing complementary equipment,” and later, it became “sufficiently general for its diffusion to be broad-based and pervasive,” so that it could reach into the Corn Belt. Farmalls were key to this second stage of tractor development because they were affordable yet versatile on the farm.
Artificial intelligence is in its Wheat Belt stage. Much like other technologies, AI is having an impact in narrow categories of work, but it has yet to be truly transformative to the broader economy. I expect that changes will come from AI, but they will take time.
Meanwhile, worker shortages persist for electricians, welders, plumbers, HVAC technicians, and other skilled trades. And as baby boomers retire en masse, the entire labor market will likely tighten, which would drive up wages on its own.
Especially in Silicon Valley, far too many people have focused on transformative AI, a utopian and unrealistic scenario where productivity grows at least 10% per year. But that means not as much attention is being paid to the near-term bottlenecks, especially since companies are struggling to turn AI into revenue.
So, what is actually happening with AI?
Economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond were the first to study the effects of a modern AI system that was rolled out to call center workers about three years ago. Their big finding was that AI substantially increased productivity. As measured by issues resolved per hour, worker productivity rose by 14% on average. But the lowest-skilled agents saw the biggest lifts from AI, a 34% increase, which was far greater than anyone at the top experienced.
This skill-equalization effect has since been found in software development, legal work, and writing. But stepping up from worker task changes to a firm-level analysis shifts the story. While improvement is often found in individual tasks, the impacts of AI on companies are far less common because they require more than just workers improving at their jobs.The few company-focused studies conducted are finding similar trends. When workers gain access to AI, their work portfolio changes. They spend less time crafting emails and more time on core tasks. In one study, software developers spent more time writing and modifying code and less time on project-management tasks. In another, employees at the National Bank of Slovakia shifted away from email and routine tasks to core job functions. For these workers, AI was complementary to nonroutine work, where judgment, synthesis, and idea generation are central to performance.
Overall, AI seems to help the workers who have the most room to improve, but it works best when tasks are not too rigid. This creates a mismatch. The employee who gains the most from AI might not be doing the kind of work where AI is most useful.
So, the real productivity gains are going to come with reorganizing work itself, moving people toward the tasks where AI can amplify their judgment, speed, and creativity. In a simulation, the study on bank employees estimated that by reshuffling workers across tasks in this way could increase total output by more than 7%.
But many companies just aren’t ready to make these kinds of changes.
Earlier this year, I collected business surveys that asked professionals about AI bottlenecks, use, and the productivity gains they’re experiencing. While these surveys should be taken with a grain of salt, managers and leaders consistently cited fragmented and low-quality data, siloed IT architectures, and persistent difficulty measuring impact alongside broader organizational change as reasons for slow AI adoption.
In general, AI adoption is advancing rapidly in experimentation, yet remains stubbornly uneven in deployment and value creation. Most firms now use AI in at least one business function. The majority are still stuck in pilots, and only a minority of companies have achieved any measurable bottom-line impact. Returns are taking years to materialize and are especially elusive for more ambitious AI systems that are trying to routinize complex tasks.
To be clear, AI is generating substantial revenues for some companies, and it is driving political conflicts at the federal and state levels. But in contrast to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, who thinks AI will eliminate 50% of all white-collar work in the next four years, AI is still only affecting a limited number of companies and a small group of workers.
Indeed, the highest-quality research on the ways that AI is shifting work, which was also led by economist Erik Brynjolfsson, has only found an impact on those aged 20-25, who are also working in software and customer service. It’s a small slice of the labor market, especially considering that mid-career workers in these segments are still seeing their wages rise. And of course, this work is not without its caveats. Ryan Nunn of The Budget Lab at Yale recently examined the microdata underlying monthly employment reports and found “no strong evidence of [AI] impacts as of yet.”
While there is a lot of talk about AI-induced job loss, the more immediate headwind is company readiness. Data must be cleaned, workflows must be redesigned, and workers have to be shifted to tasks where AI actually helps. If those complementary investments are too expensive or too difficult to manage, companies won’t integrate AI tech. In that world, adoption would look impressive in surveys but remain shallow in practice, which is exactly what we see today.
The Farmall eventually transformed farming across the Corn Belt. But it required the right conditions and farms ready to reorganize around it. AI may follow the same path. But the transformation will depend less on what the models can do in demos and more on whether firms can rebuild work around them.
